CSE 805 — Scientific interpretation of the completed Option B experiment
Learning to Predict Semantic Segmentation Errors Under Adverse Driving Conditions
Post-hoc descriptive analysis; no additional training

Central finding

Class-aware Logistic Regression improves the prespecified retrospective operating
point, but exhibits condition-dependent ranking tradeoffs. Across 17 held-out
recording proxies, mean accepted error near 80% coverage falls from 8.3934% with
MSP and 8.3514% with entropy to 7.6653% with LR + predicted class. Its mean AURC
is nevertheless worse. Night exposes a substantial low-coverage failure that
exists inside all three groups, not merely after pooling fold-specific scores.

The additional evidence supports a specific interpretation: class features alter
the ordering between predicted semantic categories. This helps when class
reliability transfers, notably for large, reliable predicted-sky populations in
Fog. It hurts when a category receives a favorable learned score despite becoming
unreliable, notably high-confidence predicted vegetation in Night. These are
observational explanations, not proof of a unique causal mechanism.

Evidence and unchanged protocol

The frozen experiment uses 1,600 labeled adverse training images, 400 per weather,
in 17 conservative filename-derived groups: Fog 6, Night 3, Rain 3, Snow 5. Each
outer fold leaves one group out; three grouped inner folds select C from
{0.01, 0.1, 1.0} by mean weighted AP. All selected C=0.01. Inner pipelines fit
preprocessing and model parameters on inner-training groups only. Final pipelines
and training-derived thresholds use outer-training groups only.
The original 406-image validation set remains separate. Filename groups are
recording proxies, not verified independent recording sessions.

The backbone is frozen SegFormer-B0. Inputs are MSP, normalized predictive entropy,
top-two margin, and optionally predicted-class one-hot encoding. The binary target
is disagreement with valid ground truth. Ground truth supplies supervision and
evaluation only. LR3 omits class; LR+class includes all 19 categories. The raw
baselines are error scores 1-MSP and normalized entropy. Normalizing entropy by a
constant does not change its ranking. No segmentation predictions are improved.

Training uses 512 uniformly sampled valid pixels/image, seed 805, with inverse
inclusion weights. Primary evaluation uses identical full valid pixels for every
method: 3,059,663,748 pixels, error prevalence 17.1147%. The primary summary gives
each held-out group equal weight; it is not the same as pooling all pixels. Primary
curves use the frozen 100,000-bin arcsine-square-root score transform, whole-bin
acceptance, and right-step AURC. That policy and the primary endpoint are unchanged.

This pass reuses all 34 fitted pipelines and saved samples. Coefficient and metric
sensitivity analyses use the same 8,192 valid-pixel QA samples/image (seed 806),
with inverse inclusion weights. Training class profiles use the original training
samples. Those estimates are explicitly distinct from full-population results.
Night/Fog class and confusion counts use all 800 images and cached native logits;
no new segmentation forward passes are needed. All outputs are isolated here.

Night prevalence differs by group: 33.93%, 29.78%, and 37.14%, respectively. Compare
AP between methods on the same group; an AP difference between weather populations
also reflects prevalence and composition. LR+class loses AUROC, AP, and AURC in
every Night group while retaining a small improvement near 80% coverage. Its Night
macro risk80 change is -0.2457 pp versus MSP, but its AURC change is +0.043643.

These results are compatible because the operating point and ranking summaries ask
different questions. At 80%, the accepted set includes many moderately scored
pixels. At low coverage, errors assigned very low scores are accepted first and
can dominate risk. AURC integrates risk over all coverage levels, so a small gain
near 80% cannot offset a large early-curve loss. AUROC and AP also assess ordering
beyond that one cutoff. The three curves in figures/night_group_risk_coverage.png
show the crossing directly.

Whole-bin policies can give different actual coverages, especially near score
clusters. A consistent diagnostic therefore uses unbinned scores on identical
weighted QA samples and the same label-independent secondary tie key for all four
methods. At almost exactly 10% coverage (within 0.00004 pp), accepted error is:

gopro_0351      1.492%     12.513%
gopro_0376      0.668%     26.454%

14.325%. These sampled post-hoc diagnostics do not replace the original endpoint;
they establish that low-coverage failure persists under a common coverage policy.

nearest 10%, actual coverage is 10.00023%, 9.99991%, and 10.00037%. Predicted
vegetation dominates acceptance and the errors within it:

The last column is also a share of ALL accepted errors. True building -> predicted
vegetation contributes a further 26.13%, 15.95%, and 19.41%. These categories are
predicted labels: the result does not mean true vegetation itself is the main
semantic source of error. No predicted-road pixel is accepted by LR+class at the
1%, 5%, or 10% requested cutoffs in any Night group. At the next requested 20%
cutoff, road enters heavily and risk is lower in all three groups. By approximately
50%, road supplies 61%–70% of accepted pixels, but risk need not keep falling:
0351 rises again after the initial decline. MSP's own near-10% sets are 98.5%–99.9%
predicted road. Its actual whole-bin coverage differs (10.046%, 9.619%, 9.306%),
which is why the common-coverage QA comparison above is also necessary.

At 80%, accepting additional vegetation errors is offset by rejecting other
classes' errors. The contribution of predicted-building errors to total accepted
risk decreases by 1.271, 1.132, and 1.567 pp in the three groups; predicted-terrain
contributions fall by 0.257, 0.338, and 0.484 pp. Vegetation contributions increase
by 0.975, 1.379, and 1.681 pp. Other classes complete the small net risk80 gain.
Each contribution divides a class's accepted errors by ALL accepted pixels;
these changes sum exactly to the total risk change, with each method's actual
denominator retained. This accounting explains how early failure can coexist
with a small improvement at the prespecified operating point.

Within-group versus pooled cross-fold ordering

The original primary comparisons are paired within each held-out group. Their
Night losses cannot be caused solely by scores from different models being pooled.
A QA pair decomposition makes the distinction explicit: 37.05% of pooled Night
positive-negative pair weight is within the same group and 62.95% crosses groups.
Within-group pair-weighted AUROC is 0.82463 for MSP versus 0.77244 for LR+class;
cross-group AUROC is 0.82017 versus 0.76814. Pooled AUROC is 0.82183 versus 0.76973.
These are pair-weighted QA diagnostics, not the equal-group primary averages.
Cross-group comparisons combine differing class/prevalence composition and
fold-specific score scales; they do not isolate a calibration defect. Pooled
curves describe cross-fitted predictions, not one deployable classifier.

What the class feature contributes

All 34 stored pipelines have verified continuous input order, scaler parameters,
and class encoding. Intercept + standardized continuous contribution + class
contribution reproduces every stored QA decision logit (maximum discrepancy
2.9e-15). At fixed continuous features, class coefficients are conditional log-odds
contrasts. They are not class error rates or causal feature importance. With the
full one-hot encoding and intercept, report centered or road-reference contrasts,
not isolated absolute category weights. MSP, entropy, and margin are correlated;
the positive conditional MSP coefficient does not mean higher confidence generally
causes more error. Adding class also refits continuous slopes and the intercept.

Night gopro_0376 is a clear reliability-transfer mismatch. Predicted vegetation
has estimated error 14.17% in the corresponding training population versus 58.46%
held out. Yet LR+class lowers its mean score from LR3's 23.68% to 20.61%. The
vegetation-versus-road class contrast is -0.6514 log-odds. For MSP>=0.99 vegetation,
training error is 3.66%, held-out error 37.49%, and mean class-aware score only
4.98%. This subset represents 5.92% of valid held-out pixels. High-confidence road
has held-out error 3.74% but a higher mean class-aware score, 8.56%. These are subset
means, not a claim about every pixel pair. The vegetation training-to-held-out error
increase also appears in the other two groups (17.03 -> 40.75%; 16.01 -> 53.00%).

The distinction between within-group and within-class ranking is important.
Within predicted road, vegetation, and building in gopro_0376, QA AUROC barely
changes from LR3 to LR+class (.9184 -> .9183, .7127 -> .7128, .6998 -> .7001).
Overall full-group AUROC falls .83179 -> .76602. A category offset is constant
within that predicted class; its direct effect is to reorder different classes.
The evidence therefore implicates cross-class ordering, while allowing refitted
slopes, feature distributions, and scene composition to contribute. Sky is only
about 0.62% of predicted valid pixels in this Night group and cannot be presumed
the dominant cause simply because its learned offset is large.

Why Fog benefits most

The mean class-aware risk80 change versus MSP is -1.4243 pp in Fog, compared with
-0.2457 Night, -0.2651 Rain, and -0.4599 Snow. Fog also improves mean AUROC, AP,
and AURC. In Fog groups 0477/0478/0479, predicted sky occupies about 36.50/38.70/
60.94% of valid pixels. Its estimated held-out error is 1.99/1.53/1.30%, close to
the corresponding training estimates 1.55/1.64/1.64%. LR3's mean sky scores
17.53/15.90/15.57% become 2.81/2.43/2.50% with class, with centered sky offsets
near -2.20. Here a large reliable category retains approximately its training
reliability and is moved toward acceptance.

Predicted buildings in those groups are much less reliable: estimated held-out
errors 74.52/62.17/94.13%. Their mean scores rise from LR3's 28.73/28.46/38.56%
to 35.74/37.69/49.39%. The scores still understate observed error; better ranking
does not establish calibrated probabilities. Within-class AUROC barely changes
in Fog0477 (sky .8020 -> .8012; building .8749 -> .8751), while overall full-group
AUROC rises .82104 -> .90703. This supports beneficial cross-class reordering.
Full-pixel counts at each Fog group's own near-80% cutoff confirm the tradeoff:
across the six groups, LR+class accepts 11.17 million fewer predicted-building
errors and 2.46 million fewer predicted-terrain errors than MSP, but 3.24 million
more predicted-sky errors. The largest removed confusion is true sky -> predicted
building (7.20 million errors). Accepting more sky is beneficial overall here,
but does not make all accepted sky predictions correct. These are sums over six
group-specific cutoffs, not a pooled single-threshold comparison or a macro metric.
Fog is not uniformly better: 0475 has slightly worse AUROC/AURC than MSP despite
better risk80, and 0480 has worse risk80. All classes and groups remain in the
exported tables, including sparse categories and unfavorable results.

Metric interpretation and sensitivity

Retrospective risk80 means choosing a threshold from the held-out score population
to approach 80% coverage and then measuring accepted error. It does not promise
80% coverage on future images or an error bound. The cutoff is not chosen by
minimizing held-out error labels, but its coverage is determined retrospectively.
The frozen whole-bin implementation may miss exact 80%; the maximum LR+class/MSP
coverage difference across groups is 0.002619 pp. The separately reported
training-derived class-aware thresholds attain only 65.65%–91.55% held-out coverage.
They are train-only thresholds, with possible optimism from using fitting data,
and provide no deployment guarantee either.

To separate binning from tie handling, the new diagnostic uses identical weighted
QA samples and common conventions: exact-score ties grouped; 10k/100k/1m transformed
bins grouped; and unbinned scores with the same stored label-independent secondary
tie keys. All grouped policies use the same right-step integration. AUROC/AP always
group true-score ties. The secondary-key variant affects selective ordering only.

Thus Night's adverse direction is stable. The frozen 100,000-bin definition understates the
unbinned class-aware AURC by about .00405 on the QA sample; exact-score tie ordering
changes it by only about .00003. LR3's much smaller AURC differences are more
sensitive to metric policy. Across all 17 groups, class-aware AURC is worse than
MSP under every tested policy; Fog remains better. This is numerical sensitivity
analysis, not a revised primary metric or a search for a favorable result.

Despite 100k bins, the largest Night class-aware bin contains 11.34%, 9.85%, or
10.87% of weighted QA mass. These bins are >99.97% predicted road and occur at
scores approximately .0744, .0847, and .0814, not at zero. Quantization merges
dense internal score clusters; this is different from genuine equal-score ties.
MSP itself has exact zero-error-score tie mass around 4.21%, 4.23%, and 6.59%, so
very low coverage is also unattainable under a whole-tie policy. The new curves
and tables always distinguish requested and actual coverage.

Contribution, cost, and limits

The student contribution is a trained error detector, feature/encoding pipeline,
grouped tuning and evaluation protocol, and measured class-feature ablation with
failure analysis. It is not a new segmentation network or a claim of publication-
level algorithmic novelty. It directly uses CSE805 preprocessing, feature
engineering, Logistic Regression, regularization, grouped validation, and metrics.
MSP and entropy use confidence summaries without an explicit predicted-category
offset; entropy is unchanged by permuting class probabilities. The experiment
tests whether learning category-conditioned reliability adds useful information.

The added computation buys a modest operating-point improvement with a serious
Night tradeoff. Recorded nested fitting/tuning takes 448.5 s. Full-population score
calls take 41.3 s for MSP, 44.7 s entropy, 188.8 s LR3, and 428.6 s LR+class, with
the recorded boundaries including validation and prediction but excluding histogram
updates. These are experimental pipeline measurements, not deployment latency.
For a course project, the class-aware method provides a clear, interpretable ML
contribution. The evidence does not support it as a universal replacement for MSP.

Only three Night proxies exist; pixels are strongly correlated. No pixel-IID
significance or population confidence claims are made. Group summaries and the
original leave-one-group-out mean ranges are descriptive sensitivity, not CIs.
Training/held-out class differences can mix weather, scene, feature distribution,
and residual recording dependence. Previous exploration makes this an empirical
study, not an untouched confirmatory trial. QA estimates, sparse classes, numerical
ties, and post-hoc choices further limit mechanistic certainty.

Future work should first verify session metadata and collect independent Night
groups, then test whether class-conditioned reliability transfers. A separate
development/calibration group could assess transferable coverage thresholds. Any
new class-by-confidence interactions, weather-aware features, or models would need
a newly specified protocol and independent evaluation. None is trained in this
pass. The most defensible final claim remains: class-aware learning improves a
prespecified retrospective operating point, with condition-dependent ranking
tradeoffs and a documented Night failure.
